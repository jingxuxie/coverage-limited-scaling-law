\begin{abstract}
Repeated passes over a fixed dataset can improve optimization, but they cannot
reveal predictive features absent from the unique examples. We formalize this
distinction in sparse power-law regression, where feature $j$ activates with
probability $p_j\asymp j^{-s}$, has covariance eigenvalue
$\lambda_j\asymp j^{-a}$, and carries target energy
$g_j\asymp j^{-b}$. Every learner incurs an unseen-feature floor
$\sum_j g_j(1-p_j)^n\asymp n^{-(b-1)/s}$. An exactly solvable one-hot
benchmark yields the three-frontier law
\[
\R(m,n,K)\asymp
m^{-(b-1)}+(\eta nK)^{-(b-1)/a}+n^{-(b-1)/s}.
\]
Our main result extends this law to genuinely multi-active data, where replay
updates do not commute. Example-norm-clipped SGD trains on every coactive row.
Natural singleton rows provide coordinate-wise curvature, while clipping makes
every rank-one update a contraction. The ordinary Polyak average attains the
coverage rate up to a logarithm without a support-abort event; a label-free,
distribution-aware anchor mask removes the logarithm and gives the sharp full
frontier. In particular, all-data replay reaches the coverage floor after
$O(\eta^{-1}n^{a/s-1})$ epochs. We also derive an input-only estimator of this
useful replay horizon. Across 795 laptop-scale training runs, experiments expose
all three frontiers, recover the predicted source exponents and scaling
collapse, and predict the saturation epoch within a median factor below $1.7$
across three spectral regimes.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}

Power-law learning curves summarize how error changes with model size, unique
data, and computation \cite{hestness2017scaling,kaplan2020scaling,
hoffmann2022training,bahri2024explaining}. They are most consequential when
one resource becomes scarce. In a data-constrained regime, additional compute
must be spent either on processing new examples or on taking more optimization
steps over examples already seen. Large-scale language-model experiments show
that a few repeated epochs can initially resemble fresh data, but the marginal
value of repetition eventually vanishes \cite{muennighoff2025scaling}. This
creates a basic resource-allocation question:
\begin{quote}
\emph{For a dataset of size $n$, how many epochs remain statistically useful?}
\end{quote}

A law expressed only through total sample updates $nK$ cannot answer this
question. Two runs can have the same update count while containing radically
different amounts of unique information. The missing distinction is especially
important for sparse or conditionally activated representations. A low-frequency
feature may be hard to learn because its covariance is small, but it may also be
completely absent from the fixed dataset. Additional epochs address the former
problem and are powerless against the latter.

Existing linear-regression scaling theory characterizes how finite model width,
sample size, and optimization time expose progressively weaker spectral
directions \cite{lin2024scaling,lin2025reuse,chen2026minibatch}. Multi-pass
results show that reuse can improve the optimization term, while one-hot
Zipfian models exhibit an eventual dataset-dependent plateau
\cite{zou2022multipass,yan2026larger}. Recent sparse-feature theory identifies
unobserved coordinates as a distinct statistical bottleneck, but studies the
solution after training to completion rather than the finite-epoch interaction
between coverage and reuse \cite{sous2026asymmetric}. Meanwhile, frequency-based
accounts of neural scaling suggest that long-tailed skills or ``quanta'' are
learned in decreasing order of occurrence \cite{michaud2023quantization}, and
recent sparse-language-model experiments report that sparsity can shift the
point at which repeated data saturate \cite{wu2026scarce}. These observations
motivate a tractable theory in which frequency, optimization difficulty, and
target importance are separately controllable.

We study sparse regression with three power-law sequences:
\[
p_j\asymp j^{-s},\qquad
\lambda_j\asymp j^{-a},\qquad
g_j\asymp j^{-b}.
\]
They respectively describe how often a feature appears, how quickly it can be
optimized, and how much population error it contributes. The resulting learning
process has three rank frontiers,
\begin{equation}
J_{\rm cap}=m,\qquad
J_{\rm opt}=(\eta nK)^{1/a},\qquad
J_{\rm cov}=n^{1/s}.
\label{eq:frontiers-intro}
\end{equation}
The smallest frontier determines the effective learned scale. This decomposition
also yields an operational rule: repeat the data while $J_{\rm opt}$ is smallest;
once it reaches $\min\{J_{\rm cap},J_{\rm cov}\}$, another epoch cannot improve
the leading error rate.

Our contributions are as follows.
\begin{itemize}
\item We prove an algorithm-independent Bayes and minimax unseen-feature lower
bound, including its sharp power-law constant. We also derive a compact
occupancy calculus for the number of observed features, the observed teacher
norm, and the missing predictive energy.
\item We derive an exact finite-$n$, finite-$K$ one-hot replay law. It exposes
the capacity, optimization, and coverage terms without an asymptotic surrogate
for the SGD dynamics.
\item For genuinely multi-active Bernoulli inputs, we resolve the
noncommuting-update obstacle for a concrete all-row algorithm. Example-norm
clipping gives pathwise contraction. Singleton rows that occur naturally in the
data provide enough curvature to prove a sharp anchor-masked bound and a plain
Polyak-average bound up to logarithms.
\item We turn the theorem into an input-only stopping rule. Regressing conditional
feature amplitude against empirical activation frequency estimates the exponent
$a/s-1$ and predicts $K_{\rm sat}$ before an expensive epoch sweep.
\item We add direct tests of all three frontiers. In 795 training runs, the
capacity sweep has slope $-0.991$ versus $-1$, the coverage slope is $-0.654$
versus $-2/3$, a two-term law explains $98.1\%$ of risk variation, and the
input-only stopping estimate has median multiplicative error below $1.7$ across
three spectral settings.
\end{itemize}

The central message is simple but not captured by total compute: epochs improve
what the dataset already contains; only unique examples move the coverage
frontier.

\section{Related Work}

\paragraph{Empirical and mechanistic scaling laws.}
Early studies documented predictable power laws across language, vision,
speech, and other modalities \cite{hestness2017scaling,kaplan2020scaling,
rosenfeld2020constructive}. Compute-optimal scaling then made these curves into
resource-allocation tools \cite{hoffmann2022training}. Theoretical accounts
connect exponents to data-manifold dimension, kernel spectra, and task--model
alignment \cite{sharma2022manifold,bordelon2020spectrum,canatar2021spectral,
bahri2024explaining}. The quantization model attributes power laws to a
Zipf-distributed sequence of discrete skills learned in frequency order
\cite{michaud2023quantization}, while the effective-frontier framework abstracts
capacity, coverage, and optimization as competing truncations of a long tail
\cite{zou2026frontiers}. Our contribution gives these mechanisms a finite-data
stochastic realization: it jointly analyzes unique occupancy and noncommuting
replay dynamics, derives the replay horizon, and supplies an input-only
estimator rather than assuming the frontier location.

\paragraph{Data constraints and repetition.}
Data-constrained language-model experiments show that modest repetition can be
nearly neutral relative to fresh data, followed by sharply diminishing returns
\cite{muennighoff2025scaling}. Recent work fits scaling laws that explicitly
include repeated tokens, data mixtures, or sparse training; notably, sparse
language models can postpone repetition-induced saturation
\cite{wu2026scarce}. These studies establish the practical importance of the
question but do not isolate which information repeated examples cannot supply.
Our contribution is a mechanism-level decomposition: $nK$ controls an
optimization frontier, whereas unique occupancy depends only on $n$.

\paragraph{Spectral regression and multi-pass SGD.}
Power-law linear regression provides sharp model--data--compute rates and a
controlled setting for studying data reuse \cite{lin2024scaling,lin2025reuse}.
Mini-batch and multi-pass extensions describe how sampling protocols and
iterations alter the optimization rate \cite{chen2026minibatch,
zou2022multipass}. Zipfian one-hot regression produces a multi-epoch saturation
law \cite{yan2026larger}; we use that commuting model as an exact benchmark and
then analyze multi-active inputs whose rank-one replay matrices do not commute.
Our proof draws on iterate averaging and least-squares SGD
\cite{polyak1992acceleration,bach2013non,defossez2015averaged,
dieuleveut2016nonparametric,neu2018averaging,ali2020implicit}, but the central
curvature argument is occupancy-driven rather than based on a uniformly
well-conditioned empirical covariance.

\paragraph{Sparse features, occupancy, and missing mass.}
Sparse-feature scaling can differ qualitatively from dense spectral theory and
can generate asymmetric interpolation phenomena \cite{sous2026asymmetric,
belkin2019reconciling,hastie2022surprises}. Mathematically, our observed
feature dictionary is an infinite-urn process \cite{karlin1967central}. The
unseen predictive term is a weighted missing mass, related to classical
concentration results for unseen symbols and occupancy counts
\cite{berend2013missing,benhamou2017concentration}. We use regular variation not
only to evaluate the missing mass, but also to couple it to the observed teacher
norm that drives finite-time optimization.

\paragraph{Clipping and heavy-tailed optimization.}
Rare activations can create large conditional feature amplitudes even when the
population covariance is trace class. Gradient clipping is widely used to
control such heavy-tailed or locally unstable updates, and prior theory gives
high-probability convergence guarantees under heavy-tailed noise
\cite{gorbunov2020clipping,zhang2020attention}. Our clipping rule has a more
specific role: it enforces $\gamma_i\|X_i\|^2\le1$, making every replay update a
Euclidean contraction. This permits a transparent comparison between
coactivation, singleton curvature, and feature occupancy.

\section{Sparse Power-Law Regression}

For $j\ge1$, let
\begin{equation}
X_j=q_jZ_jE_j,\qquad
Z_j\sim{\rm Bernoulli}(p_j),\quad E_j\sim{\rm Rad},
\label{eq:model}
\end{equation}
independently over coordinates and examples. Then
$\lambda_j=\E X_j^2=p_jq_j^2$. Labels are noiseless,
$Y=\langle\theta,X\rangle$, and the predictive energy of coordinate $j$ is
$g_j=\lambda_j\theta_j^2$. We assume
\begin{equation}
\begin{aligned}
p_j&\asymp j^{-s},\qquad
\lambda_j\asymp j^{-a},\qquad
g_j\asymp j^{-b},\\
s&>1,\qquad a\ge s,\qquad b>1.
\end{aligned}
\label{eq:powerlaws}
\end{equation}
The condition $s>1$ makes every example finitely supported almost surely. The
condition $a\ge s$ makes $q_j^2=\lambda_j/p_j\asymp j^{-(a-s)}$ nonincreasing,
which permits a rank-independent stable base step. Population excess risk is
\[
\R(w)=\E_X[\langle w-\theta,X\rangle^2]
=\sum_j\lambda_j(w_j-\theta_j)^2.
\]
For an arbitrary measurable predictor, we use
$\R(f)=\E_X[(f(X)-Y)^2]$.

\subsection{Phase boundaries and experimental design}

The pairwise boundaries make the regime diagram directly testable. Capacity and
coverage coincide when
\begin{equation}
m\asymp n^{1/s};
\label{eq:cap-cov-boundary}
\end{equation}
capacity and optimization coincide when
\begin{equation}
\eta nK\asymp m^a;
\label{eq:cap-opt-boundary}
\end{equation}
and optimization reaches coverage when
\begin{equation}
K\asymp\eta^{-1}n^{a/s-1}.
\label{eq:opt-cov-boundary}
\end{equation}
These identities suggest three complementary sweeps. Varying $m$ at large $nK$
isolates the capacity exponent; varying $K$ at fixed $n$ reveals optimization
followed by a plateau; varying $n$ at large $K$ measures the coverage exponent.
A convincing empirical test should therefore change one frontier while keeping
the other two safely larger. The experiments below follow exactly this design.

The exponent geometry also separates two notions that are easy to conflate.
The target exponent $b$ determines how much error remains beyond a learned rank,
and hence changes every risk slope. By contrast, the epoch at which reuse
saturates depends only on the input-side ratio $a/s$: $a$ controls how weak the
rare directions are, while $s$ controls how quickly unique examples discover
them. This is why the stopping horizon can be estimated from inputs without
labels, even though the height of the final error floor remains task dependent.

Let $D_N$ contain $N$ unique examples and define the observed dictionary
$\cS_N=\cup_{i\le N}{\rm supp}(X_i)$, its size $V_N=|\cS_N|$, the observed
Euclidean teacher energy $A_N=\|\theta_{\cS_N}\|_2^2$, and the missing
predictive energy $U_N=\sum_{j\notin\cS_N}g_j$. The following proposition makes
explicit the three occupancy quantities used throughout the proof.

\begin{proposition}[Occupancy calculus]
\label{prop:occupancy}
Assume exact profiles $p_j=c_pj^{-s}$, $\lambda_j=c_\lambda j^{-a}$,
and $g_j=c_gj^{-b}$. Let
\[
\alpha=\frac{a-b+1}{s},\qquad
\beta=\frac{b-1}{s}.
\]
If $a-s+1<b<a+1$, then $0<\alpha<1$ and
\begin{align}
\E V_N
&\sim \Gamma(1-1/s)(c_pN)^{1/s},
\label{eq:EV}\\
\E A_N
&\sim \frac{c_g}{c_\lambda}
\frac{\Gamma(1-\alpha)}{a-b+1}(c_pN)^\alpha,
\label{eq:EA}\\
\E U_N
&\sim \frac{c_g}{s}\Gamma(\beta)(c_pN)^{-\beta}.
\label{eq:EU}
\end{align}
For two-sided power laws, the same quantities are respectively
$\Theta(N^{1/s})$, $\Theta(N^\alpha)$, and $\Theta(N^{-\beta})$.
\end{proposition}

The identities follow by summing the inclusion probabilities
$1-(1-p_j)^N$ or exclusion probabilities $(1-p_j)^N$ and applying a rescaled
Riemann-sum argument. The source interval has a concrete meaning. Its lower
endpoint makes the observed Euclidean norm integrable at the coverage scale;
its upper endpoint ensures that this norm actually grows with $N$. Moreover,
\begin{equation}
\alpha+\beta=\frac as,
\label{eq:alpha-beta}
\end{equation}
which is exactly the identity that later converts the finite-time optimization
term into the saturation exponent $a/s-1$.

For the information-theoretic lower bound, take independent Rademacher signs for
the teacher coordinates while fixing their magnitudes.

\begin{theorem}[Coverage floor]
\label{thm:coverage}
Every measurable, possibly randomized learner trained on $N$ unique examples
satisfies
\[
\E_{\theta,D_N}\R(\widehat f)
\ge \sum_{j\ge1}g_j(1-p_j)^N
\asymp N^{-(b-1)/s}.
\]
For exact profiles, the leading constant is the one in
Eq.~\eqref{eq:EU}. The same rate is a minimax lower bound over deterministic
choices of the teacher signs.
\end{theorem}

\paragraph{Why the lower bound is algorithm independent.}
If coordinate $j$ is absent from every training input, no observed label depends
on its teacher sign. Conditional on the full dataset, all other teacher signs,
and the learner's randomness, that sign remains symmetric. Averaging squared
test error over the hidden signs leaves at least the unseen predictive energy.
No amount of optimization, regularization, or replay can reduce this term.

\section{Exact Replay Benchmark}

In the one-hot benchmark, example $i$ chooses $J_i\sim(p_j)$ and
$X_i=E_iq_{J_i}e_{J_i}$. A width-$m$ model is trained from zero for $K$ passes
with step $\eta$. Let $C_j\sim{\rm Binomial}(n,p_j)$ count unique occurrences of
coordinate $j$. The update matrices now commute, so the complete finite-sample
risk is explicit.

\begin{proposition}[Exact one-hot replay]
\label{prop:onehot}
If $0<\eta q_j^2<1$, then
\begin{equation}
\E_{D_n}\R
=
\sum_{j\le m}g_j
[1-p_j+p_j(1-\eta q_j^2)^{2K}]^n
+\sum_{j>m}g_j.
\label{eq:onehot-exact}
\end{equation}
Under Eq.~\eqref{eq:powerlaws},
\begin{equation}
\E\R\asymp J_\star^{1-b},\qquad
J_\star=\min\{m,(\eta nK)^{1/a},n^{1/s}\}.
\label{eq:threefrontier}
\end{equation}
Equivalently, the risk is within constants of
\[
m^{-(b-1)}+(\eta nK)^{-(b-1)/a}+n^{-(b-1)/s}.
\]
\end{proposition}

Indeed, each occurrence multiplies the residual coefficient by
$1-\eta q_j^2$, and the binomial probability-generating function gives
Eq.~\eqref{eq:onehot-exact}. The effective survival exponent is
\[
np_j\{1-(1-\eta q_j^2)^{2K}\}
\asymp \min\{np_j,\eta nK\lambda_j\}.
\]
The first argument asks whether the feature appears; the second asks whether it
has received enough optimization. Balancing the two at the coverage rank gives
\begin{equation}
K_{\rm sat}\asymp\eta^{-1}n^{a/s-1},
\label{eq:ksat-onehot}
\end{equation}
independent of the source exponent $b$. Proposition~\ref{prop:onehot} is a sharp
benchmark, but its diagonal updates avoid the main difficulty in multi-active
data.

\section{Coactive Replay SGD}

For $N$ selected examples, a sparse dictionary stores every identity in
$\cS_N$ whenever $V_N\le m$. On the exponentially unlikely capacity-overflow
event the algorithm returns zero; otherwise the selected problem is exactly
realizable. At each update, sample a stored row uniformly with replacement and
use
\begin{equation}
\begin{aligned}
\gamma_i&=\min\left\{\eta,\frac{\rho}{\|X_i\|_2^2}\right\},\\
w_{t+1}&=w_t-\gamma_iX_i(X_i^\top w_t-Y_i).
\end{aligned}
\label{eq:clippedsgd}
\end{equation}
where $0<\rho\le1$, followed by
$\bar w_T=T^{-1}\sum_{t<T}w_t$. We assume
$\eta\sup_jq_j^2\le\rho$. Every sampled coactive row is used; singleton examples
appear only in the analysis.

Let $C_{N,j}$ count examples whose support is exactly $\{j\}$. Because
$\sum_jp_j<\infty$,
\begin{equation}
\begin{aligned}
\pi_0&=\prod_{k\ge1}(1-p_k)>0,\\
\Pr\{{\rm supp}(X)=\{j\}\}
&=\frac{\pi_0p_j}{1-p_j}\asymp p_j.
\end{aligned}
\label{eq:singleton-probability}
\end{equation}
Fix $0<\kappa\le\pi_0/2$ and define the label-free, distribution-aware anchor
set and output
\begin{equation}
\cA_N=\{j:C_{N,j}\ge\kappa Np_j\},
\qquad
\widehat w_T=P_{\cA_N}\bar w_T.
\label{eq:anchor}
\end{equation}
The mask is applied only at readout and never filters a training row.

\begin{theorem}[Sharp coactive replay and plain output]
\label{thm:coactive}
Assume $a-s+1<b<a+1$ and $N\le c_mm^s$. Then example-norm-clipped,
with-replacement replay satisfies
\begin{equation}
\E\R(\widehat w_T)
\le C\left[
\frac{N^\alpha}{\eta T}+N^{-\beta}+e^{-cm}
\right].
\label{eq:anchor-bound}
\end{equation}
The ordinary unmasked Polyak average, with no singleton-support abort or zero
fallback other than capacity overflow, satisfies
\begin{equation}
\E\R(\bar w_T)
\le C\left[
\frac{N^\alpha}{\eta T}
+\{\log(eN)\}^{a/s}N^{-\beta}
+e^{-cm}
\right].
\label{eq:plain-bound}
\end{equation}
The expectation is over the unique dataset and replay indices.
\end{theorem}

\subsection{Proof anatomy}

The proof separates stability, empirical curvature, and occupancy. Let
$\delta_t=w_t-\theta_{\cS_N}$. Since
$\gamma_i\|X_i\|^2\le1$, every rank-one update contracts pathwise:
\begin{equation}
\|\delta_{t+1}\|^2
\le \|\delta_t\|^2-\gamma_i(X_i^\top\delta_t)^2.
\label{eq:contraction}
\end{equation}
This is the point at which clipping resolves rare, high-norm coactive examples:
they can slow an update but cannot increase the Euclidean error.

A singleton-$j$ row is not clipped under the base-step assumption. Conditional
on the fixed dataset, average Eq.~\eqref{eq:contraction} over the next replay
index, retain only singleton contributions, and telescope. This gives the
energy inequality
\begin{equation}
\frac{\eta}{N}\sum_{t<T}\E_{\rm sgd}
\sum_j C_{N,j}q_j^2\delta_{t,j}^2
\le A_N.
\label{eq:energy}
\end{equation}
On $\cA_N$, the coefficient satisfies
$C_{N,j}q_j^2/N\ge\kappa p_jq_j^2=\kappa\lambda_j$. Jensen's inequality for the
Polyak average therefore converts Eq.~\eqref{eq:energy} into
\[
\E_{\rm sgd}\sum_{j\in\cA_N}\lambda_j
(\bar w_{T,j}-\theta_j)^2
\le \frac{A_N}{\kappa\eta T}.
\]
For the masked coordinates, a Chernoff bound gives
$\Pr(j\notin\cA_N)\le e^{-cNp_j}$, hence
\[
\E\sum_{j\notin\cA_N}g_j
\lesssim\sum_jj^{-b}e^{-cNj^{-s}}
\asymp N^{-\beta}.
\]
Finally, Proposition~\ref{prop:occupancy} supplies
$\E A_N\asymp N^\alpha$, proving Eq.~\eqref{eq:anchor-bound}.

The plain output uses the same energy inequality on a simultaneous-coverage
core
$J_N\asymp(N/\log N)^{1/s}$. On the represented tail,
pathwise contraction implies
$\sum_{j>J_N}\lambda_j\bar\delta_j^2
\le\lambda_{J_N+1}A_N$. Taking expectations gives
\[
\lambda_{J_N+1}\E A_N
\asymp \{\log(eN)\}^{a/s}N^{-\beta},
\]
where Eq.~\eqref{eq:alpha-beta} is essential. A weighted occupancy-moment bound
and H\"older's inequality control the rare datasets on which the core lacks
singleton anchors. Thus no abort event is built into the plain algorithm. The
supplement gives all constants, the moment lemma, and the capacity-overflow
bound.

\begin{corollary}[All-data saturation]
\label{cor:alldata}
With $N=n$, $T=nK$, and $m\gtrsim n^{1/s}$,
\[
\E\R(\widehat w_{nK})
\lesssim \frac{n^{\alpha-1}}{\eta K}+n^{-\beta}.
\]
Consequently,
\begin{equation}
K_{\rm sat}=O(\eta^{-1}n^{a/s-1})
\label{eq:ksat-sharp}
\end{equation}
epochs suffice to reach the information-theoretic coverage rate. The plain
average obeys the same power law up to the logarithm in
Eq.~\eqref{eq:plain-bound}.
\end{corollary}

\begin{corollary}[Full frontier]
\label{cor:full}
Given $n,m,T$, select
\[
N_\star\asymp\min\{n,m^s,(\eta T)^{s/a}\}
\]
unique examples and run anchor-masked clipped replay. Then
\begin{equation}
\begin{aligned}
\E\R(\widehat w_T)=O\bigl(&m^{-(b-1)}
 +(\eta T)^{-(b-1)/a}\\
&+n^{-(b-1)/s}+e^{-cm}\bigr).
\end{aligned}
\label{eq:full-frontier-rate}
\end{equation}
The unmasked output attains the same exponents up to logarithms.
\end{corollary}

The progressive subset is a conservative achievability construction rather
than a claim that discarding data is optimal. At fixed update compute, the
experiments consistently favor more unique examples.

\subsection{Budgeted Data Acquisition and Replay}

The preceding corollaries treat unique examples and updates as independently
specified resources. A more operational question is how to divide one budget
between acquiring fresh examples and replaying the examples already available.
Let one unique example cost $c_{\rm d}>0$, let one SGD update cost
$c_{\rm u}>0$, and impose
\begin{equation}
 c_{\rm d}N+c_{\rm u}T\le B.
 \label{eq:budget-constraint}
\end{equation}

\begin{corollary}[Budget allocation]
\label{cor:budget}
For a sufficiently small constant $c_0$, choose
\begin{align}
N_B&=\left\lfloor c_0\min\left\{
 n,m^s,(\eta B/c_{\rm u})^{s/a},B/c_{\rm d}
 \right\}\right\rfloor,\nonumber\\
T_B&=\left\lfloor\frac{B-c_{\rm d}N_B}{c_{\rm u}}\right\rfloor.
\label{eq:budget-schedule}
\end{align}
Then anchor-masked clipped replay satisfies
\begin{align}
\E\R(\widehat w_{T_B})=O\bigl(&m^{-(b-1)}+n^{-(b-1)/s}
 +(\eta B/c_{\rm u})^{-(b-1)/a}\nonumber\\
&+(B/c_{\rm d})^{-(b-1)/s}+e^{-cm}\bigr).
\label{eq:budget-rate}
\end{align}
The unmasked output has the same power laws up to logarithms.
\end{corollary}

The derivation makes the resource tradeoff transparent. The constant $c_0$
ensures $T_B\asymp B/c_{\rm u}$, and the choice of $N_B$ balances the replay
term $N^\alpha/(\eta T)$ against the coverage term $N^{-\beta}$, using
$\alpha+\beta=a/s$. Thus, when $n$ and $m$ do not bind and $a>s$,
\begin{equation}
 N_B\asymp(\eta B/c_{\rm u})^{s/a},\qquad
 K_B=T_B/N_B\asymp B^{1-s/a}.
 \label{eq:budget-interpretation}
\end{equation}
Only a sublinear number of new examples is required as the budget grows; most
additional budget is eventually spent replaying them until optimization meets
coverage. When $a=s$, the two scales are both linear and fresh data must retain
a constant budget share. The schedule is independent of $b$: target smoothness
changes how much error lies beyond a learned rank, whereas $a$ and $s$ determine
where the resource frontiers cross. Equation~\eqref{eq:budget-rate} is a
frontier-matched guarantee for this benchmark, not a universal claim that the
same cost split is minimax for arbitrary representations or noisy tasks.

\section{An Input-Only Replay Stopping Rule}

Corollary~\ref{cor:alldata} implies that the useful number of epochs depends on
$a$ and $s$, but not on $b$. This is not merely an exponent-level observation.
For exact profiles,
\begin{equation}
q_j^2=\frac{\lambda_j}{p_j}
=c_qp_j^{a/s-1},\qquad c_q=c_\lambda c_p^{-a/s}.
\label{eq:q-vs-p}
\end{equation}
The coverage frontier consists of features with $p_j\asymp1/n$. Such a feature
appears only $O(1)$ times per epoch and needs
$K\eta q_j^2\asymp1$ to be optimized. Therefore
\begin{equation}
K_{\rm sat}\asymp\frac{1}{\eta q^2(p=1/n)}.
\label{eq:ksat-input}
\end{equation}

This identity suggests a label-free plug-in estimator. For every feature that
appears at least $r_{\min}$ times, estimate its activation frequency
$\widehat p_j$ and its conditional second moment $\widehat q_j^2$. Fit
\[
\log\widehat q_j^2=\widehat c+\widehat r\log\widehat p_j
\]
and predict
\begin{equation}
\widehat K_{\rm sat}
=\eta^{-1}\exp(-\widehat c)n^{\widehat r}.
\label{eq:stopping-estimator}
\end{equation}
The theorem assumes known $p_j$ only for the sharp output mask; the stopping
estimator itself uses observed inputs alone. A full plug-in theorem for unknown
activation probabilities is left open, but the experiment below tests the
finite-sample calibration directly.

\section{Experiments}

\subsection{Setup}

We generate the independent Bernoulli design exactly in sparse CSR form by
first sampling each coordinate occupancy and then assigning its active rows.
Unless noted otherwise,
\[
p_j=\frac{\mu}{\zeta(s)}j^{-s},\qquad
\lambda_j=j^{-a},\qquad g_j=j^{-b},
\]
with $(a,s,b)=(2,1.5,2)$, expected active features $\mu=1$, $\eta=0.2$,
clipping radius $\rho=0.9$, and ambient dimension $d=4096$ or $8192$. Risk in
the simulated coordinates is computed exactly from the coefficient error and
we add the analytic tail $\zeta(b,d+1)$. The primary protocol is clipped
with-replacement replay. All main sweeps use five seeds. The original release
contains 670 runs; the capacity and stopping diagnostics add 125 runs, for 795
runs and 5,400 recorded risk checkpoints in total.

\subsection{Optimization and coverage frontiers}

Figure~\ref{fig:main} shows power-law optimization followed by a floor that
moves only with unique data. At $n=8192$, the early averaged-risk slope is
$-0.459$, close to the prediction $-(b-1)/a=-0.5$. At $K=512$, the slope across
dataset sizes is $-0.6548$, versus $-(b-1)/s=-0.6667$. Fitting all 60 primary
averaged-risk points to
\begin{equation}
A(\eta nK)^{-(b-1)/a}+Bn^{-(b-1)/s}
\label{eq:two-term-fit}
\end{equation}
gives $R^2=0.981$ and median relative error $9.5\%$, without fitting either
exponent. The normalized curves collapse around
$\eta Kn^{1-a/s}\asymp1$, precisely the saturation scale in
Eq.~\eqref{eq:ksat-sharp}.

No experiment uses a singleton-support abort. The anchor mask is evaluated only
as a conservative, label-free readout diagnostic; headline exponents and model
comparisons use the ordinary unmasked output. In finite samples the plain output
is consistently better because it retains features seen only in coactive rows,
while Theorem~\ref{thm:coactive} guarantees it up to a logarithm. Thus the mask
is a sharp-proof device rather than an empirical performance trick.

\subsection{Capacity frontier}

We isolate $J_{\rm cap}$ by oracle rank-aligned truncation: at each $m$, set
$d=m$, train every coordinate, and add the analytic target tail to test risk.
Unlike fixed-ambient masking, no omitted component adds training-label noise.
With $n=32768$, $K=256$, and $m\in\{64,128,\ldots,4096\}$, the
Polyak-risk and target-tail slopes for $m\le256$ are $-0.991$ and $-0.996$,
matching $m^{-(b-1)}=m^{-1}$. Near $m=1024$, the capacity tail falls below the
total finite-width unseen-feature floor, which is then coverage-dominated.

\subsection{Input-only stopping prediction}

We use $r_{\min}=8$ in Eq.~\eqref{eq:stopping-estimator}. The measured stopping
point is the first log-interpolated epoch at which the averaged risk is at most
$1.25$ times the exact unseen-feature floor. We test
$(a,s,b)=(2,1.5,2)$, $(2.4,1.5,2)$, and $(2.5,2,2.5)$ over six dataset sizes.
The mean fitted exponents $\widehat r$ are respectively
$0.349$, $0.629$, and $0.257$, compared with the true values
$0.333$, $0.600$, and $0.250$. The median multiplicative errors in
$\widehat K_{\rm sat}$ are $1.68$, $1.49$, and $1.55$. These errors include both
estimation noise and the finite-grid definition of ``near the floor.'' The
right panel of Figure~\ref{fig:diagnostics} shows that the estimator nevertheless
tracks the change in replay horizon across data scales and spectral regimes.

\paragraph{Unique data at fixed compute.}
At $T=2^{18}$ updates, increasing unique examples from $256$ to $32768$ lowers
last-iterate risk by a factor of $12.8$, even though total sample updates are
unchanged. This directly rejects a law depending on $nK$ alone after coverage
becomes active.

\paragraph{Why clipping matters.}
Across expected coactivation $\mu\in[0.35,2.35]$, clipped cyclic, reshuffled,
and with-replacement replay approach the same floor. Under the aggressive
setting $(\mu,\eta)=(2.25,1.2)$, unclipped reshuffling reaches mean risk
$1.16\times10^{85}$ by four epochs and later overflows. Clipped replay remains
stable and reaches $7.08\times10^{-3}$, near its
$6.18\times10^{-3}$ coverage floor. This stress test directly supports the
pathwise mechanism in Eq.~\eqref{eq:contraction}.


\subsection{Diagnosing the Active Frontier from Local Slopes}

The frontier law also gives a scale-local diagnostic.  For
\begin{equation}
L(m,T,N)=C_m m^{-\delta}+C_T(\eta T)^{-\gamma}+C_NN^{-\beta},
\label{eq:elasticity-proxy}
\end{equation}
with $\delta=b-1$, $\gamma=(b-1)/a$, and $\beta=(b-1)/s$, define
$e_x=-\partial_{\log x}\log L$.  Direct differentiation gives
\begin{equation}
\frac{e_m}{\delta}+\frac{e_T}{\gamma}+\frac{e_N}{\beta}=1,
\label{eq:elasticity-simplex}
\end{equation}
and each normalized elasticity is exactly the fraction of predicted risk due
to that resource.  Thus the largest normalized local slope identifies the
active bottleneck without knowing $C_m,C_T,C_N$.  The pairwise
optimization--coverage boundary satisfies $T\asymp\eta^{-1}N^{a/s}$, or
$K\asymp\eta^{-1}N^{a/s-1}$, recovering the saturation law.

The measured width, update, and data slopes ($-0.991$, $-0.459$, and
$-0.655$) approach the three simplex vertices; the complete derivation and a
finite-difference allocation rule appear in the supplement.

\section{Discussion and Limitations}

The saturation epoch predicts when optimization reaches the unique-data
frontier, not the exact validation optimum in every noisy application.  Label
noise may create an earlier variance- or overfitting-limited optimum, so our
coverage horizon is an upper limit on useful unregularized reuse.  The sharp
anchor mask uses population $p_j$; estimating it from small counts requires
additional uniform-control arguments.  The plain Polyak output avoids this
mask and any support-abort event, at the logarithmic cost in our present proof.

The theorem assumes addressable independent sparse coordinates,
with-replacement replay, clipping, averaging, noiseless labels, and
$a-s+1<b<a+1$.  Sketches or learned features can mix rare directions, while
cyclic replay, reshuffling, and last iterates have different dynamics; their
sharp guarantees remain open.  Progressive subsampling is only an achievability
construction.  Still, distinct-feature growth and conditional moments are
observable, yielding a falsifiable rule: replay while optimization is active,
then redirect resources to unique data or model capacity.

\section{Conclusion}

Sparse data impose a resource that total updates miss: unique feature coverage.
We derived its unavoidable floor, its interaction with capacity and optimization,
and the epoch scale at which stable coactive replay reaches it. Width sweeps and
an input-only estimator validate all three frontiers. The decision rule is
simple: replay while optimization is active; once coverage dominates, collect
unique data or expand the model.
